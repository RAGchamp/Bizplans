Honest verdict: the pipeline is buildable in one off-season, but time savings alone barely pay for it. In the base case the firm frees about 10,700 hours a year, yet only $417k of that becomes real money. After the subscription and running costs, $55k is left, and the $222k of staff time spent on setup takes about four years to earn back. The stronger reasons to buy are proof of what each answer relied on and faster response when tax law changes, and neither is counted in that figure.

The firm is invented. Only the client, fee and staffing figures come from the brief. All other figures are this page's planning assumptions, not measurements, and need testing with a real firm.

2. The Pipeline at This Firm

This is the same four-step design as Plan 15, filled in with tax work.

Firm sources: about 40,000 documents

Prior returns, K-1s, depreciation schedules, S-corp and accounting-method elections, operating agreements, IRS and state notices, planning memos, engagement letters. Plus public tax law for 12 states.

1. Compiler: 158,000 facts

Reads each document once and pulls out facts such as "Entity 3 is 60% owned by Owner A." Sends conflicts, such as two different carryforward balances, to a named reviewer.

2. Ledger: one record of what is in force

Stores each fact with its source passage, its dates, its authority and which client team may see it. Old versions are kept.

3. Answer Receipts: about 138,000 a year

The research assistant, email drafts and return sign-off all check against the Ledger and log the facts they used.

4. Recall Engine: when law or client facts change

Traces a changed fact back to the affected clients, ranks them, and alerts the owner, re-runs the answer or escalates to a director.

Sizing: 100 clients × 400 knowledge-bearing documents, leaving out bank statements and receipts. 150,000 client facts (1,500 per client), 6,000 tax-law facts and 2,000 firm position facts. Receipts: 60 fee-earners × 10 answers a day × 230 days.

The legal gate sits here. Return data is protected. A contractor that receives it is itself treated as a tax return preparer under Section 7216, must get written notice of that, and may receive only what the contracted service needs.3 Disclosure to a contractor outside the US needs taxpayer consent.3 Tax firms also need a written information security plan under the FTC Safeguards Rule.5 6 The contractor paragraph of the regulation is written around software and equipment services, and this page's searches found no IRS guidance on generative AI, so counsel must confirm how an AI vendor fits before any client data moves. The design assumes US-only hosting and removes Social Security numbers at ingest. Outside counsel's fees are not included.

Done when: 200 test questions return the right answer "as of" any chosen date, and a test finds no cross-client leaks.

5. A Real Law Change as the Test

Plan 15's example uses an invented memo. Tax has a real one: the 2025 bonus depreciation change. The dates and rules below are real. Client C-047, its oven line and the client counts are invented. Rates assume ordinary property placed in service in 2025.

MomentStandard RAGFactLedger
July 1, 2025: what is the bonus rate?News articles on the pending bill can outrank the law and return 100%, which is not yet law.Returns 40% under the existing phase-down.2 A pending bill is not a fact in force.
After July 4, 2025: the law passesThe newest text says 100%, so that is applied to everything.Splits by acquisition date: 100% for property acquired after January 19, 2025,1 and still 40% for property acquired earlier.2 The old fact narrows instead of closing.
October 7, 2025: Michigan decouples8The federal answer is reused for the state return.Adds a separate Michigan fact, based on federal law as of December 31, 2024.4 Clients with Michigan filings are flagged.
January 14, 2026: IRS Notice 2026-11The notice is one more document to find.Adds the interim guidance, including the option to elect 40% (or 60% for certain property) in place of 100%.1 Clients with open decisions are flagged.
Who was affected?Managers review client lists by memory and spreadsheet.Recall traces 640 receipts across 62 clients, then narrows to 37 clients who bought property after January 19: 9 high priority (advice already sent or estimates already paid), 19 medium, 9 low.
Why this makes a good acceptance test: the firm already handled this change by hand, so the correct client list exists. Replay the change on its 2025 files and compare. If Recall misses clients that staff found, or floods reviewers with false alerts, the firm learns that before relying on it. This test could run during the $60,000 Knowledge Audit on about 10 clients.

7. What This Means for Plan 15

FindingWhy it mattersSuggested change
The price list has a gapThis use case needs fact-level Recall. The plan includes that only in the $500k+ Enterprise tier, which loses money here in the base case. The $150k Domain tier stops at document-level Recall.Test a professional-firm tier near $150k–$250k with fact-level Recall for one domain.
The first customer loses moneyFactLedger spends $512k to win $250k in the first year. Only the next firms benefit from the $336k of reusable work, and each still costs about $176k to deploy.Enter tax only with a plan to sell several firms, or cut deployment effort with templates.
Tax law can be sharedThe 6,000 tax-law facts are the same for every firm. Compiled once, they could be sold to many firms at low cost.Consider a shared tax-law Ledger as a lighter product. Check licences first: compile public primary sources, not paid research content.
Two dates are not enoughTax law can reach backward: a July 2025 law changed the rate for property bought from January 20.Add "applies to" as a third date in the Section 5 receipt design.
Receipts depend on behaviorThe plan assumes agents answer. Here, people do much of the work in tools that leave no trail.Track receipt coverage as a headline metric alongside the plan's grounded-answer share.
Recommendation: keep banks and insurers as the first market, as the plan says. Treat tax firms as a later segment, and only with lower deployment cost. A $10M firm should start with the Audit and the replay test on about 10 clients, then commit to the full build only if the replay works and the firm can name who will own the Ledger.
