Executive Summary

Small retailers — an independent home-goods store, a regional apparel chain, a specialty hardware shop, anyone selling both in a physical location and online with annual revenue under $10 million — run sales campaigns constantly: seasonal clearances, Black Friday, back-to-school, a slow-Tuesday promo. Every one of those campaigns comes down to the same unanswered question: how deep should the discount be? Go too shallow and barely move inventory; go too deep and give away margin the extra volume never earns back. Enterprise retailers answer this with dedicated pricing-science teams and six-figure analytics platforms. A retailer doing $2–8 million a year has neither — just a store owner's gut feeling and a spreadsheet.

MarginIQ is a Gross Profit Simulator, not a generic dashboard: a retailer enters a product's cost price, current selling price, and a few candidate discount percentages, and MarginIQ scores real customer response data with a trained machine-learning model to predict expected purchasers, revenue, and gross profit for each option — then recommends the one that actually maximizes profit, which is frequently not the deepest discount. This is not a hypothetical: the core mechanic is already built and running end to end on real (synthetic-but-realistic) sales data, with a worked example showing exactly this — a $400-cost, $1,100-selling-price product where a 25% discount beat both a 10% and a 40% option on expected gross profit (full trace in Grounding Example).

Retailers get 90 days completely free to run the Gross Profit Simulator against their own upcoming campaigns before paying anything — the trust-building step this price-sensitive, analytics-skeptical buyer needs. After the trial, the product converts to one simple, flat subscription:

200 paying retailers × $5,000/year ≈ $1,000,000 in Year-1 revenue — a deliberately modest sliver of a directional 150,000–250,000-retailer addressable market (see Target Market).

Because retailers will not switch analytics tools mid-holiday-rush, the plan is calendar-aware: trials launch in the off-peak months so 90 days of runway lands squarely before Q4 campaign planning, when the Gross Profit Simulator's answer matters most (see Go-to-Market Strategy). Against an estimated $400,000 in total Year-1 spend, this lands at an estimated ~$600,000 pre-tax profit and roughly ~$420,000 after an assumed ~30% tax rate (see Net Profit Model).

2. The Problem

#ProblemWhy it hurts
1Every sales campaign is a real bet on an unknown number. A retailer picking between 10%, 25%, and 40% off is really picking between three different expected-profit outcomes — but has no way to see any of them in advance.The decision gets made on gut feel or "what we did last year," not on how customers with this specific price point, product, and segment are actually likely to respond.
2Deeper discounts feel safer but aren't. A bigger discount reliably drives more transactions, so it looks like the right call in the moment.More transactions at a thinner margin can easily produce less total profit than a shallower discount that sells less but keeps far more of each sale — a tradeoff that's invisible without actually modeling it.
3Enterprise pricing-science tools are built for a different customer. Dynamic-pricing and pricing-intelligence platforms exist, but are priced, scoped, and integration-heavy for retailers with dedicated merchandising and data teams.A retailer doing $2–8 million a year has neither the budget nor the staff to operate that class of tool — the analytics gap tracks directly with company size, not need.
4Basic POS/e-commerce analytics answer the wrong question. Square, Shopify, and Lightspeed all report what sold.None of them predict what would sell at a discount depth that hasn't happened yet — the forward-looking question a campaign decision actually requires.
5Generic BI/dashboard tools require a data analyst to operate. Tableau or Power BI can chart historical sales, but building a predictive, per-scenario profit model on top requires skills this segment doesn't have on staff.The gap isn't "no data" — most retailers have a POS system generating plenty of it — it's nobody on staff who can turn that data into a forward-looking recommendation.

There is currently no product built specifically to turn a small retailer's own sales history into a profit-optimal discount recommendation, at a price and a level of hand-holding this segment can actually use. That gap is the business opportunity.

3. The Product — Four Core Capabilities

What it is

A working web application — not a slide deck — built around four core capabilities:

A. Campaign & Customer Response Tracking
Ingests a retailer's own campaign and sales-interaction history (or starts from a guided sample) so every past campaign's real conversion rate and revenue is visible in one place — the baseline every prediction is grounded against.
B. The Gross Profit Simulator (the flagship capability)
Enter a product's cost price, selling price, target customer segment, channel, and a handful of candidate discount percentages; a trained machine-learning model scores real customer response data per scenario and recommends the one that maximizes expected gross profit — not just the one that sells the most units.
C. Model History & Time Travel
Every trained version of a retailer's own prediction model is tracked (built on MLflow, the open-source ML tracking standard), so a retailer can see how the model — and its recommendations — evolved as more campaigns ran, and retrain on demand as new sales data comes in.
D. Guardrails & Plain-English Explainability
Every recommendation comes with a warning if the discount falls outside anything the model has actually seen before, and a feature-importance breakdown of what actually drove the prediction — so a non-technical owner can trust and audit the number instead of taking a black box on faith.
Already built, not a roadmap promise

Capability B — the Gross Profit Simulator — is not a concept; it is a working feature in a real Flask + scikit-learn + MLflow application today, scoring real (synthetic) customer response data through a trained GradientBoostingClassifier and returning a genuine, non-trivial recommendation. See Grounding Example for the actual output. The Year-1 build plan (Build Plan & Roadmap) is about productizing this into a secure, multi-tenant SaaS for many retailers at once — not building the core mechanic from zero.

User journey — planning one campaign
  1. The retailer connects their sales data (POS/e-commerce integration, or a guided CSV import during onboarding).
  2. Ahead of a campaign, they enter the product's cost price, current selling price, target segment/channel, and 2–5 candidate discount percentages.
  3. The Gross Profit Simulator scores every eligible customer at each discount level and computes expected purchasers, revenue, cost, and gross profit per scenario.
  4. The highest-profit scenario is flagged as recommended — with a plain-English explanation and a warning if it's an extrapolation beyond anything the model has seen.
  5. After the campaign runs, the real results feed back into the model, so the next recommendation is grounded in one more real campaign's worth of evidence.

One number a store owner can actually act on — before the campaign goes live, not after

4. Grounding Example — A Real Worked Campaign

To make "predicts the profit-optimal discount" concrete rather than a pitch-deck abstraction, this plan is grounded against a real output from the actual, already-running application — not a hypothetical. A furniture retailer scenario: a product costing $400 to source, selling at $1,100, targeted at the Gold-tier customer segment, campaign running both in-store and online, with three candidate discounts on the table: 10%, 25%, and 40%.

The Gross Profit Simulator scored 25 real eligible customers against the currently trained model and returned:

DiscountPredicted PurchasersConversion RateRevenueCostGross Profit
10%2.5910.4%$2,562$1,035$1,527
25%4.9819.9%$4,108$1,992$2,116Recommended
40%5.0020.0%$3,299$2,000$1,300

Going from 10% → 25% off, predicted purchasers nearly double (2.59 → 4.98) while margin per unit only shrinks moderately — profit rises. Going from 25% → 40% off, predicted purchasers barely move at all (4.98 → 5.00 — this audience is already saturated near 20% conversion), but margin keeps shrinking — profit falls. The middle option wins, and the tool shows exactly why — precisely the kind of counter-intuitive-but-correct answer a store owner cannot get from a spreadsheet or a gut feeling, and precisely the demo moment the 90-day free trial (Product Structure & Trial) is built to deliver in a prospective customer's first session.

5. Target Market

Primary buyer/user: the owner or general manager of an independent or small-chain retailer selling through both a physical store and an online storefront, with annual revenue roughly $1M–$10M — large enough to run a real, recurring sales-campaign calendar, small enough to have zero dedicated analytics or data-science headcount.

Segment sizing (directional)
Of the well over a million small retail establishments tracked in U.S. Census County Business Patterns data, the narrower slice combining an omnichannel (store + online) presence with $1M–$10M in revenue is estimated directionally at 150,000–250,000 businesses — reachable through POS/e-commerce platform marketplaces, independent-retailer associations, and retail trade events.
Secondary buyer (future)
Franchise groups and multi-location independent chains at the higher end of the range (approaching $10M+), where the same per-campaign discount decision is made across more locations and more products, raising the value of a consistent, explainable recommendation.

Not the target audience (out of scope for Year 1): large national retail chains with existing enterprise pricing-science teams and dynamic-pricing platforms; pure online-only sellers with no physical-store channel complexity (a simpler problem, and a more crowded existing tool market); retailers running fewer than 2–3 discretionary sales campaigns a year, for whom the ROI case is thin.

6. The Campaign-Planning Transformation — From Gut Feel to Data-Backed Decisions

This is the center of the business case, not a side effect of the software: MarginIQ does not ask a small retailer to hire anyone or change how they run their store — it changes what happens in the fifteen minutes before a campaign goes live.

MomentBefore MarginIQWith MarginIQ
Picking the discountGut feel, "what we did last year," or defaulting to the deepest discount that still feels safeA ranked comparison of 2–5 real scenarios by predicted gross profit, not just predicted units sold
Justifying the decisionNo way to explain the choice beyond intuition — hard to defend to a business partner or lenderA plain-English, feature-level explanation of what drove the recommendation, plus a warning if it's outside anything the model has seen
Learning from the campaignTotal sales get noticed; whether the discount depth itself was the right call rarely gets analyzedPredicted vs. actual response is tracked automatically, sharpening every future recommendation

MarginIQ tells them what's about to happen, for a decision they were already going to make with or without data — the discount depth on the next campaign. That's the difference between a nice-to-have report and a tool that changes the actual number on the receipt.

8. Why the Pricing Works

  • $5,000/year is a flat, per-business fee — not per seat, not a percentage of revenue — because the buying unit here is a single owner-operated business, not a team of analysts. Budget-predictable pricing matters enormously to a buyer who has never had a software line item this size before.
  • The math pays for itself inside one good campaign. The worked example in Grounding Example shows an $816 gross-profit swing between the recommended option and the deepest discount — on a single product, in a single campaign, for one 25-customer segment. A retailer running 4–8 campaigns a year across their full catalog has many multiples of that opportunity on the table; capturing even a fraction of it clears the $5,000 fee many times over.
  • Priced well below the enterprise pricing-science tools this segment can't access anyway — dynamic-pricing and pricing-intelligence platforms built for large retailers are typically priced and scoped for teams with dedicated merchandising staff, putting them out of reach regardless of interest. $5,000/year sits inside what a small retailer already spends on basic software tools (POS fees, e-commerce platform fees, email marketing).
  • The 90-day free trial de-risks the sale for a skeptical buyer. Ninety days is long enough to run at least one real seasonal campaign through the Simulator and see the recommendation borne out (or not) before any money changes hands — the trust-building step this analytics-wary segment needs, not just a lead-gen gimmick.

13. Competitive Landscape

  • Enterprise dynamic-pricing / pricing-intelligence platforms — genuinely capable, but priced, scoped, and integration-heavy for retailers with dedicated merchandising and data teams; a non-starter for a $2–8M retailer regardless of interest.
  • Generic BI/dashboard tools (Tableau, Power BI, and similar) — powerful at charting what already happened, but require a data analyst on staff to build anything predictive on top; this segment doesn't have one.
  • Built-in POS/e-commerce analytics (Shopify, Square, Lightspeed reporting) — good at "what sold," not built to answer "what would sell at a discount depth that hasn't happened yet."
  • Gut feel + spreadsheets (status quo) — the real competitor for nearly every prospective customer; the pitch is a better decision on a bet they were already making, not a feature comparison.

Differentiation: the combination of a purpose-built profit-optimization mechanic (not just a dashboard), a price point this segment can actually afford, and a self-serve, guided onboarding designed for someone with no analytics background is not something any of the above alternatives offer together.

15. Strategic Advantage / Moat

  • The model compounds per retailer. Every campaign a retailer runs feeds real results back into their own model, so recommendations get more accurate against that retailer's actual customer base over time — a moat that deepens with usage, not just with tenure.
  • The model history becomes a renewal anchor. A retailer's tracked campaign and model history (built on MLflow) lives inside the platform; switching away means losing that institutional memory, not just a subscription.
  • Explainability builds trust a black box can't. Guardrails and feature-importance transparency are core to the product, not an afterthought — a real edge with a buyer who is inherently skeptical of "just trust the AI."
  • Cheap to run, priced to match. The core mechanic is a lightweight scikit-learn model, not LLM-inference-heavy — unit economics stay strong at a price point (~$475 cost per acquired customer against $5,000 in first-year revenue) that enterprise-focused competitors have no reason to chase downmarket to match.
  • Distribution through platforms the buyer already trusts. Marketplace presence inside Shopify and Square puts MarginIQ where this exact buyer already looks for tools, ahead of any competitor relying solely on outbound sales.

19. Summary

MarginIQ turns the one campaign decision every small retailer makes over and over — how deep should this discount be — from a gut call into a modeled, explainable recommendation, using a Gross Profit Simulator that is already built and already produces real, non-obvious answers (see Grounding Example). Priced as a single flat $5,000/year subscription after a genuinely full-featured 90-day free trial, the product is built for a buyer enterprise pricing-science tools have always priced out: an omnichannel retailer under $10 million in revenue with real sales data and no one on staff to turn it into a forward-looking answer. Because that buyer's biggest campaigns cluster around Q4, the go-to-market plan is deliberately calendar-aware — trial cohorts timed to convert ahead of the holiday season, distributed through the POS/e-commerce marketplaces this buyer already uses rather than a costly outbound sales motion. Hitting 200 paying retailers for roughly $1,000,000 in Year-1 revenue, against an estimated $400,000 in total Year-1 spend, lands at an estimated ~$600,000 pre-tax profit and ~$420,000 after an assumed ~30% tax rate — a strong outcome for a narrowly-targeted, self-serve product addressing a real, recurring, and currently unmet decision every small retailer already has to make.
