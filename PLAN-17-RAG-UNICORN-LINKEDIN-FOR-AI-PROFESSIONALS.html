1. Executive Summary

The fundamental problem. Knowledge is becoming a commodity. When anyone can ask an AI, knowing a framework or a syntax proves little. The skill that now separates IT professionals is AI amplification: how well they understand LLMs, how efficiently they direct them, and how good the final result is. Résumés and profiles were built to show job titles and listed skills, and they cannot show this.

The product. Amplifolio is a customer-facing app with one job. A professional picks a Starter Template, a realistic problem with hidden tests and planted traps. They solve it with whatever AI tools they like. A Proof Run records how they did it: result, cost, time and the errors they caught. The solution then goes on a public portfolio, where others can learn from it.

The key idea. A portfolio made with AI can be made entirely by AI, so an ordinary project gallery proves nothing. Amplifolio therefore runs each template with AI alone first. Human Lift measures how much better the person did than that baseline. That number is what "AI amplifier" actually means, and it can be compared because everyone faced the same problem.

The gap. LinkedIn launched verified AI skills in January 2026, but those certificates come from individual tool vendors and reflect usage inside each tool.1 CodeSignal tests agentic coding, but for employers during a hiring process.3 This plan's searches found no public, tool-neutral portfolio that compares professionals on identical problems. That was a web search, not proof of absence, and Stage 0 must retest it.

Business model in one line: professionals build and share for free, can pay $144 a year for Pro features, and employers pay about $32,000 a year to search and contact verified talent. Employers provide most of the revenue.
Honest verdict: this is the cheapest of the three unicorn plans to test, and the hardest to win. It faces LinkedIn, a two-sided cold start that Polywork did not survive, and a score that must predict real job performance or employers will not pay. This plan estimates about a 1% chance of reaching $1B, the lowest of Plans 15–17. The downside is limited, though: Stage 0 can be done solo for about $20,000, and a small paid community near the site's $175k scale is a realistic fallback.

2. The Problem — Profiles Cannot Show Amplification

Stack Overflow is one sign of how fast knowledge is losing its scarcity value. Monthly questions have fallen about 99% from their 2014 peak, although that decline began well before ChatGPT.10 Meanwhile, in a March 2026 survey cited by CodeSignal, 91% of US engineers said they use agentic coding tools, and 56% said they would hesitate to hire or work with an engineer who cannot.3 Using AI is becoming the baseline. The open question is who uses it well.

Gap 1: Claims are free

Anyone can type "GenAI" or "prompt engineering" into a profile, and AI can polish the wording.

Listing a skill says nothing about the quality of what the person produces with it.

Gap 2: Nobody can compare

Every GitHub project solves a different problem at a different difficulty, so two portfolios cannot be ranked fairly.

A clean repository also hides whether the person did the thinking, or whether an agent did.

Gap 3: Self-belief is unreliable

In METR's 2025 trial, experienced developers took about 19% longer with AI, yet believed it made them about 20% faster.4

One small study on early-2025 tools, and METR's later data points toward a speedup, weakly.4 The lesson is that people misjudge their own AI gains, so this has to be measured.

A refinement to the thesis: this plan does not claim that amplification is the only skill left. Coverage of PwC's 2026 AI Jobs Barometer stresses that human skills still top the list,5 and CodeSignal's assessment still asks engineers to explain their decisions to a person.3 The strongest version of the idea is that amplification is judgment: knowing what to ask, what to distrust and when to stop. So Amplifolio measures judgment, not prompt tricks.

5. The Product & How It Works

1. Starter Template

A real-world problem with data, hidden tests, a cost budget and planted traps. Already run by AI alone to set a baseline.

2. Proof Run

The professional solves it with any AI tool. A lightweight recorder captures prompts, agent steps, cost and time.

3. Amplification Score

Hidden tests, trap detection, efficiency and a written rationale combine into a score and Human Lift.

4. Portfolio

A public page with the solution, score and approach, plus a card to share on LinkedIn.

5. Community and employers

Others learn from retired solutions and fork them. Employers search by proven ability, not keywords.

Starter Templates: real problems with something to catch

Every template needs a trap, a mistake that a capable AI makes by default. Catching it is the clearest sign of judgment.

The four hard problems
  • Predictive validity. A high score must mean better work on the job. If it does not, nothing else in this plan matters.
  • Template decay. Each stronger model can solve more, so templates need constant renewal.
  • Fair scoring. LLM judges can favor certain writing styles or languages, so the scored parts should rely on tests wherever possible.
  • Privacy. Recordings must never capture employer code or personal data, which is one reason to use synthetic templates.
Why members return when not job hunting
  • Learning: study how top scorers solved a retired template.
  • Status: monthly leaderboards by role, tool and region.
  • Staying current: new templates when models change.
  • Share value: proof cards that perform well on LinkedIn.
  • Team use: companies benchmark and upskill their own staff.
Product sequence: each step works without the next
ReleaseWhat shipsWhy people adopt it

What changedWhy it matters
AI skills pay. Coverage of PwC's 2026 barometer reports a 62% wage premium across more than a billion job ads.5People have a strong financial reason to prove this skill. Other studies find a smaller premium, so the direction is firmer than the number.
Interviews are changing. Meta, Google and Canva are reported to be testing or allowing AI in some interview rounds, though policies vary and reports conflict.14Employers now want to see AI use, not ban it, which needs new evidence.

7. Target Market

Beachhead: mid-career IT professionals retooling for AI

The first job: "I know I am good with AI. Help me prove it in a way a hiring manager will believe." Start with solutions architects, data engineers, backend engineers and QA leads, in the US and India.

These people have real experience, feel the threat, and can act quickly. Students come later, because they have more time but less judgment to show. The first employers are AI-native startups and consultancies: they hire fast, they care about this skill, and they do not need long procurement. The catch is that these buyers are small, so large contracts take years.

8. Business Model & Pricing

Professionals pay little or nothing, because their profiles are what attract employers. Employers pay because they save screening time and reach proven people. Members use their own AI subscriptions, so Amplifolio does not fund their model usage.

Why an employer could pay $32,000

Buyer-reported LinkedIn Recruiter Corporate seats run about $8,000–$12,000 a year.12 A blended Amplifolio contract equals about three of those seats.

If an employer makes 4 hires a year, that is $8,000 per hire, far below a typical agency fee of 20% of salary (an assumed benchmark).

The catch: hiring is rare

2,500 employers making 4 hires each is 10,000 hires a year. With 1.2M active members, only about 1 in 120 would be hired through the platform each year.

So jobs alone cannot retain members. Learning, status and team use have to do that.

A possible upside, not in the model: verified professionals could be offered paid expert work for AI labs and enterprises, with a marketplace fee. Mercor's reported scale shows this is large, but it is a different, lower-margin and more crowded business.9

Stage 0 takes six months, needs one person, and is assumed to cost $15,000–$25,000 for compute, legal review and small participant payments. Each test exists to end the plan cheaply if the thesis is wrong. Plans 15 and 16 need the same six months, so these would have to run one after another.

The floor: the solo fallback. If Tests 1–3 pass but employers will not pay, this can still be a paid learning community. 900 Pro members at $195 a year is about $175,000, matching the site's usual scale and Plan 11's price. First 30 days

  4. Publish the first results and a free share card, and see whether strangers want to try.

Use LinkedIn, do not fight it

Polywork pitched itself against LinkedIn and could not match its network of bosses, colleagues and alumni.7 Amplifolio should be a proof layer that people link to from LinkedIn. Each shared result is free distribution. "LinkedIn for AI professionals" is a helpful description, but a poor strategy.

14. Strategic Advantage / Moat

A startup has no moat on day one. These advantages build up only if the company survives long enough, roughly in the order listed.

Comparable history

Every run adds to a dataset of how humans and AI perform on identical problems over time. That record cannot be recreated later, and it also shows how each new model changes the value of human work.

Validity evidence

Linking scores to real job outcomes takes years of data. Once published, this is what makes a score trusted, and it is hard to copy quickly.

Neutrality

A tool vendor can only certify its own tool. An independent scorer can compare them all, which is the one thing LinkedIn's partners and OpenAI cannot credibly do.

Portable reputation

Members own their proofs in an open format. That sounds like a weakness, but it builds trust after Triplebyte's candidates lost theirs,8 and a widely accepted format strengthens the network.

17. Summary

As knowledge becomes cheap, the valuable skill is directing AI well, and judging its work. Today's profiles cannot show that, and an ordinary portfolio cannot prove it, because AI can produce the portfolio too.

Amplifolio's answer is simple: give everyone the same real problem, measure what AI does alone, and show what each person adds. Starter Templates make the skill comparable, Proof Runs make it credible, and public portfolios make it visible. The hard parts are proving the score predicts real work, getting past the cold start, and staying ahead of LinkedIn.

Next step: build three templates, test them with ten engineers and interview fifteen hiring managers. Then let the five kill tests decide. If they pass, this becomes a venture-scale network with a roughly $1B target. If employers will not pay, it may still support a solo community. Either way, the next six months are affordable.
